Which law do I apply?

Anchor on the basis period or transaction date. Periods and events up to 31 December 2025 follow CITA and the Finance Acts; those from 1 January 2026 follow the NTA. The Study Text's own examples do exactly this: a vehicle bought in 2024 keeps its CITA initial allowance, while one bought in March 2026 goes straight-line under the NTA (Modakeke). The May 2026 model answer still charged tertiary education tax for the 2026 year of assessment because its basis period ended in June 2025.

Expect mixed-period questions

A Question 1 that spans 2024 to 2027 will need two rulebooks: initial and annual allowances then straight-line classes; tertiary education tax then development levy; a separate 10% CGT then gains inside total profit. Label each year with the law you are applying. It earns marks and protects you if the marker reads the transition differently.

Format & technique

How the paper is built

Section A · 30 marks

One compulsory question. A company income tax computation over several years (adjusted profit ~7–13 marks, tax liabilities ~11–24 marks) plus 3–6 marks of theory: back duty, pioneer rules, dividend exemptions, interest restriction.

Section B · 2 of 3 × 20

Reliably one petroleum computation. Usually one international question (DTR, non-resident, treaty, TP) and one discussion question.

Section C · 2 of 3 × 15

Capital gains with Finance Act commentary, mining, and discussion topics (ethics, practice, audit, policy, BEPS, ESG).

3 hours 15 minutes including 15 minutes' reading time; answer five of seven. Before November 2021 Question 1 carried 40 marks. Syllabus weights: A Tax administration 5% · B Companies & investment income 35% · C International 20% · D Current issues 10% · E Tax management & planning 10% · F Extractive industries, the balance (20%).

The answer frame ICAN marks
  1. Letterhead or memo heading, date, addressee, RE: line. Marking guides award ½ mark each for heading and introduction.
  2. One-paragraph introduction tied to the brief.
  3. A summary table of results (tax per year of assessment).
  4. Narrative answers to the theory parts, one point per mark.
  5. Appendices: computations with year of assessment and basis period in every heading; workings numbered.
  6. Close with an offer of further clarification and a signature.
Marks hide in the workings
  • Model answers give ½ mark per correctly treated line item. A wrong add-back costs only ½ mark; an omitted one costs the same, so list everything you adjusted.
  • Write the basis period beside each year of assessment. The 2021 and 2024 reports both cite wrong basis periods as the commonest error.
  • For theory, "1 mark per point, maximum n points." Give n + 1 crisp points, not three long paragraphs.
  • Show fiscal price × barrels × exchange rate for every petroleum revenue line.

Chapter 1

National Tax Policy 2017 and the canons of taxation

In plain words

A tax policy is the government's stated thinking behind its taxes: what the tax system is for and the principles every tax must respect. Nigeria's is written down in the National Tax Policy 2017, which applies to all three tiers of government. The canons of taxation are the classic tests of a good tax: fair, certain, cheap to collect and convenient to pay. In the exam this topic is theory only: you list and explain principles, objectives and who is responsible for what.

Canons are the qualities of a good tax: equality (ability to pay, with horizontal and vertical equity), certainty (Adam Smith: time, manner and amount known in advance), economy (low collection cost), convenience, productivity (Bastable: a sizeable yield that does not hamper production), elasticity (yield responds to need), simplicity and diversity.

The NTP defines tax as any compulsory payment to government imposed by law without direct benefit or return of value, whatever it is called. Its five objectives: guide the operation and review of the system; provide the basis for future legislation and administration; be a reference point for stakeholders; set benchmarks for accountability; clarify roles and responsibilities.

Seven fundamental features (NTP guiding principles)
  1. Equity and fairness
  2. Simplicity, certainty and clarity
  3. Convenience
  4. Low compliance cost
  5. Low cost of administration
  6. Flexibility
  7. Sustainability

Hook: Every Simple Convenience Lowers Long-run Fiscal Strain. Asked for five in Nov 2023.

Tax system, wealth creation & employment
  • No retroactive tax laws
  • Equal opportunity for local and foreign investors
  • Long-term, tenured investment policy
  • Incentives broad, sector-based, tenured, transparent and reviewed
  • Revenue forgone quantified and reported annually
  • No policy that creates monopoly or entry barriers

Mandates of the three tiers, implementation measures, and fiscal–monetary–trade interaction

Mandates: a nationwide TIN drawing on BVN, NIMC, NCC, CAC, FRSC and NIS data; voluntary compliance through simple self-assessment, amnesty frameworks and education; technology-led collection; automated record keeping; information sharing; enforcement of civil and criminal sanctions; adequate funding including for refunds; ease of payment; autonomy; amicable dispute resolution before litigation.

Implementation measures: budget speeches carry fiscal policy statements; one revenue agency per tier; a tax court; taxation committees in the legislatures; an establishment Act for the JRB; lower small-business rates and a VAT threshold; a tax policy implementation committee; an office of tax simplification (target: top 50 for ease of paying taxes); a tax policy website; reports to the National Economic Council; a national tax day; INEC to require parties to publish tax agendas.

Interaction of policies: fiscal policy (Ministry of Finance: spending, taxation, borrowing through the budget), monetary policy (CBN: money supply, interest rates, credit) and trade policy (tariffs, exchange rate, openness) share the long-run goal of growth with price stability. Fiscal dominance arises when deficits force the central bank to monetise debt.

Chapter 2

Tax organs, taxing rights and multiple taxation

In plain words

Tax administration is the machinery that collects tax: who may tax what, and which agency does the work. Nigeria is a federation, so the Constitution splits taxing powers: the Federal Government taxes companies, States tax resident individuals, and local governments collect rates and levies. Where tiers overlap, the same person pays several charges on the same base, which is called multiple taxation. From 2026 the Nigeria Revenue Service (NRS) replaces the FIRS, and the Joint Revenue Board coordinates all the tax authorities.

AuthorityTaxes it administers under the NTAA 2025

NRS and JRB in one glance
  • NRS: Executive Chairman appointed by the President with Senate confirmation; six Executive Directors, one per geopolitical zone (4 + 4 years); a Board with ex-officio members (Finance, Planning, AGF, Petroleum, CBN, RMAFC, Customs, CAC) and a Technical Committee.
  • JRB: chaired by the NRS Executive Chairman with every State IRS and the FCT-IRS chairman. Keeps the national TIN database, settles residency disputes between tax authorities, advises on treaties and new taxes, publishes revenue and tax expenditure data, guides accreditation of tax agents. The TAT and the Tax Ombud sit under the JRB Act.
Fiscal federalism (pitfall May 2021)

Fiscal federalism is the system of revenue generation and redistribution among the component units of a federation, and the financial functions and responsibilities each tier carries. Its base is s.2(1)–(2) of the Constitution: one indivisible sovereign state, a federation of States and a Federal Capital Territory. Other federations: the USA, Canada, Australia, India, Germany, Brazil, Switzerland, Malaysia.

Causes of multiple taxation
  • Autonomous taxing powers of all three tiers
  • Unfair revenue sharing; fiscal imbalance pushes States and LGs to raise levies
  • Levies with no legal backing
  • Same base taxed twice (VAT vs State consumption tax; CIT plus several profit levies)
  • Weak harmonisation and enforcement of the approved list
  • Private consultants and touts paid on commission
Impact on compliance & yield
  • Investment uncertainty
  • Erosion of the tax base as businesses relocate
  • Disincentive to file and pay correctly
  • Lower yield and higher cost of collection
  • Higher compliance cost, more disputes
Solutions
  • Harmonised taxes and levies per tier (NTAA; States Harmonised Taxes & Levies Bill 2025)
  • One revenue agency per tier (NTP)
  • JRB coordination and dispute settlement
  • Ban on consultants and touts collecting tax
  • Single-window, technology-based payment
  • Tax Ombud complaints route
  • 2023 Executive Orders (Nov 2024 question)

Chapter 3 · NTAA ss.34–55, JRB Act 2025

Objections, appeals, the Tax Ombud and refunds

In plain words

An assessment is the tax authority's formal demand for tax. A taxpayer who disagrees follows a strict ladder: object to the authority first, then appeal to the Tax Appeal Tribunal, then go to the courts on points of law. Each step has a deadline, mostly 30 days, and missing one makes the assessment final. The NTAA 2025 adds two softer routes: negotiated settlement (ADR) and complaints to the Tax Ombud.

Alternative dispute resolution

Allowed at any stage where it serves revenue or public policy, litigation cost outweighs benefit, the taxpayer accepts the authority's position, or a whistleblower settlement exposes schemes. Not for deliberate evasion or fraud, or where a court ruling would improve compliance. Ends in a signed written agreement (issues, undertakings, future-year treatment, withdrawal of appeals, payment), enforceable as a debt.

Assessments & refunds
  • Self-assessment is a deemed assessment, even nil (s.34)
  • Best-of-judgment where no return (s.35)
  • Additional assessment within 6 years; no limit for deliberate misstatement (s.36)
  • Tax paid in the currency of the transaction
  • Refund within 90 days of decision; claim within 6 years (s.55)
Pitfalls on this topic
  • Nov 2025 Q7(d) asked for objection and appeal procedure: candidates gave the old FIRS Act sequence. Use the NTAA timeline above, and mention the 90-day deemed acceptance.
  • Appeal from the TAT to the Federal High Court is on points of law only. Model answers stress it.
  • A "report to your client on presenting a case before the TAT" wants three stages: pre-hearing (file notice, pay fee, serve, response), hearing (opening, evidence, cross-examination, closing), post-hearing (judgment, service, appeal).

Chapter 4 · NTA ss.22–24

Basis periods: commencement, cessation, change of date

A company is Nigerian if incorporated under CAMA 2020 and is taxed on its worldwide profits. A foreign company is taxed on profits attributable to its Nigerian operations. Since the Finance Act 2019, and retained in s.22 NTA, every year is on preceding year basis: there are no overlaps and no double-counting at commencement.

In plain words

Companies are taxed for a year of assessment, which is a calendar year, but each company makes up accounts to its own year end. The basis period links the two: it is the set of accounts whose profit is taxed in a given year of assessment. The normal rule is preceding year basis, so accounts ending in 2026 are taxed in the 2027 year of assessment. Special rules apply when a business starts, stops or changes its year end, and a wrong basis period throws every later figure off.

Change of accounting date to 2025 (CITA)
  1. Find the first tax year affected by the change
  2. Take it and the next two years

  4. Compute again on the new date
  5. Total each set
  6. Assess the higher total; later years follow the new date

Change of accounting date from 2026 (s.23 NTA)

The basis period for the year of change runs from the day after the previous basis period to the new accounting date, whatever its length. Later years follow the new date. Notify the NRS at least 30 days before the usual filing due date.

Chukwuma Ltd moves from December to June 2026: 2027 YOA = 1/1/26 – 30/6/26 (six months); 2028 YOA = 1/7/26 – 30/6/27.

Post-cessation receipts and payments

Treated as received or paid on the last day before cessation (Enugu Ltd: bad debt recovered +₦20,000, rent paid −₦40,000). New in s.24 NTA: disclose them to the NRS within one month; resulting additional assessments or repayments carry normal objection rights.

Chargeability

A company is charged in its own name; through its principal officer, attorney, agent or representative; or, in receivership or liquidation, in the name of its receiver or liquidator. From 2026 a Nigerian parent is also taxed on the undistributed profits of a controlled foreign company (CFC rule) and must top up a low-taxed foreign subsidiary to the minimum effective rate.

Pitfalls
  • May 2024 Q1: wrong basis periods for a company that commenced in 2018. A first year of assessment that fell before the Finance Act 2019 still uses the old rule; later years use preceding year basis. The Study Text shows the resulting gap year (Olubiyi: no 2020 assessment).
  • May 2021 Q2: commencement rules not applied to the 2nd and 3rd years after a pioneer period. The day after the pioneer period is a new trade.

Chapter 5 · NTA ss.20–21, 163–164, 192

Adjusted profit: what you add back and what you take out

Start from net profit per accounts. Add expenses that are not deductible and taxable income left out; deduct income that is not taxable and deductible expenses not charged. Under the NTA an expense must be incurred wholly and exclusively in producing the income; the CITA test also demanded "necessarily and reasonably".

In plain words

Accounting profit is not taxable profit. The tax law refuses some costs (depreciation, fines, reserves) and exempts some income (franked dividends, government bond interest), so you start from profit before tax and adjust it. Adding back the refused costs and taking out the exempt income gives adjusted profit, which for a continuing company is its assessable profit. This is step one of every company tax question and the step examiners say candidates get wrong most often.

Allowable (s.20 NTA)
  • Interest on debt used to earn the income, within the connected-person cap
  • Rent and premiums on premises used for the business
  • Salaries, wages and staff benefits
  • Repairs and renewals of premises, plant, implements and utensils
  • Costs of establishing, preserving or defending title to an asset
  • Contributions to approved pension schemes
  • Proven loss or damage of stock
  • Bad and doubtful debts (not with related parties; doubtful debts per GAAP; evidence of recovery efforts)
  • Research and development, capped at 5% of turnover
  • Pre-commencement costs within 6 years, deemed incurred on day one
  • REIC dividends and mandatory distributions
  • Compensating payments that qualify as interest in a regulated securities lending transaction
  • Assistive devices and disability-related products
  • Donations to qualifying bodies within 10% of profit before tax
Disallowable (s.21 NTA)
  • Capital repaid or withdrawn; any capital expenditure
  • Domestic or private expenses; assets not used in the business
  • Amounts recoverable under insurance or indemnity
  • Taxes on income or profit, in Nigeria or abroad
  • Payments to unapproved pension or provident funds
  • Depreciation, impairment, and unrealised exchange differences
  • Sums reserved out of profits (general reserves, general provisions)
  • Payments to connected persons not at arm's length
  • Expenses of earning exempt income; costs deductible against a chargeable gain
  • Fines and penalties under any law
  • Tax or penalty borne for another person (WHT you absorbed for a landlord)
  • Dividend-type compensating payments in securities lending
  • Any expense on which VAT was due but not charged, or imports on which duty or levy was not paid

Chapter 7 · NTA First Schedule

Capital allowances

Capital allowances replace depreciation so every taxpayer writes off assets at the same statutory rate. The company must own the asset at the end of the basis period and use it in the business.

In plain words

Depreciation is an accounting estimate each company chooses for itself, so the tax law ignores it and gives capital allowances instead: a fixed, statutory write-off of the cost of business assets. Qualifying capital expenditure (QCE) is the cost of an asset that earns allowances, and tax written-down value (TWDV) is the part of that cost not yet written off. Allowances are deducted after adjusted profit to reach total profit, so they reduce tax without changing the accounts. From 2026 the NTA writes assets off straight-line in three classes; older assets follow transition rules.

Chapter 6 · NTA s.27

Loss relief

In plain words

A tax loss arises when a company's allowable expenses exceed its taxable income for a basis period. The company does not get a refund; it carries the loss forward and sets it against later profits of the same trade until it is used up. In the computation, losses come off after assessable profit and gains, and before capital allowances.

Companies
  • Carry forward only, against profits of the same trade, first available year onward, until used. No time limit.
  • Never relieve more than the loss.
  • Compute the loss on the same basis period as assessable profit.
  • No current-year relief (that is for individuals, claimed within 12 months) and no terminal loss relief.
  • Order in the computation: assessable profit + chargeable gains − losses b/f − capital allowances = total profit.
New ring-fence

A loss from selling or disposing of digital or virtual assets is deductible only against profits from the digital-asset business. Petroleum losses are ring-fenced by class (PML vs PPL) for hydrocarbon tax; see Oil & gas.

Pitfall: Nov 2020 Q2

Loss brought forward and donations treated in the wrong order in a petroleum computation. Losses come off adjusted profit to give assessable profit; allowances then come off assessable profit.

Chargeable gains

The Capital Gains Tax Act is repealed. A company's chargeable gains are now added to total profit and taxed at 30%, on preceding year basis (a disposal in the 2026 accounts falls into the 2027 year of assessment).

In plain words

A chargeable gain is the profit a company makes when it sells or otherwise disposes of an asset such as land, shares or plant, as distinct from its trading profit. Until 2025 it was taxed separately at 10% under the Capital Gains Tax Act. From 2026 the NTA adds the gain to total profit, so it bears the normal 30%. The gain is the proceeds less the asset's cost, or its TWDV if allowances were claimed, less incidental costs, subject to exemptions and reliefs.

Chapter 9 · NTA ss.4, 9–10, 19, 162; NTAA s.51

Investment income: dividends, interest, royalties, rent, REICs

In plain words

Investment income is what a company earns from holding investments rather than trading: dividends, interest, royalties and rent. Most of it suffers withholding tax (WHT): the payer deducts tax and pays it to the tax authority. For a Nigerian company WHT is usually an advance payment of its own tax, but on dividends from Nigerian companies it is final. That is why such dividends, called franked investment income, are taken out of the computation.

Dividends
  • Income = the gross dividend, arising on the day it becomes payable.
  • Dividend received after WHT is franked investment income: final tax, excluded from total profit. On redistribution, the WHT suffered offsets WHT due.
  • Scrip or bonus shares: not taxable and no WHT.
  • To a non-resident: WHT is final, and neither payer nor recipient can reclaim it.
  • Exempt: from authorised collective investment schemes; from wholly export-oriented businesses; foreign dividends brought in through approved channels.
  • Old CITA exemptions: dividends out of pioneer profits; dividends on foreign-currency equity of 10%+ for 5 years (agriculture, petrochemicals, LNG) or 3 years (others).
Interest, royalty, rent
  • Interest is Nigerian when the payer is a Nigerian resident or a Nigerian PE. The NTA definition is wide: penal interest, discounts, profit share in non-interest finance, the finance-cost element of a finance lease, FX differences on debt and securities, hedging payments.
  • Exempt: foreign interest, royalty and rent brought in through approved channels (CBN or authorised dealers).
  • Rent received in advance is spread over the period, up to five years.
  • Allowable against rent: rates and land use charge, collection and agency costs, adverts for tenants, repairs, bad debts, interest on loans for the property, insurance, water rate. Not: depreciation, capital costs, appropriations.
Real estate investment companies

An SEC-approved REIC is exempt on dividends and rent received for its shareholders if it distributes at least 75% within 12 months of year end. Distributions are deductible and outside dividend tax. Not exempt: the shareholders on what they receive; the REIC's own fees and income; anything when the 75%/12-month test fails. Illustration 9.2: 86% distributed within six months, so ₦240m is excluded and the dividend-tax base of ₦260m is below total profit.

Withholding tax mechanics

s.51 NTAA and the Deduction of Tax at Source (Withholding) Regulations 2024: deduct when payment is made or settled; between related parties, at payment or when the liability is recognised, whichever is earlier. Tax-exempt payers must still deduct. The Study Text uses 10% on dividends, interest, rent and royalties to non-treaty residents and 7.5% for treaty residents. Small companies' income is not subject to WHT.

Pitfalls
  • May 2022 Q1: the CITA conditions for exempting dividends.
  • May 2023 Q1(c): the tax consequences of dividends for the company and its shareholders. Cover both sides.
  • May 2024 Q3(c): WHT for a recipient in a non-treaty country (10%, final) against a treaty country (7.5%).

Chapter 12

Taxation of leases

IFRS 16 puts nearly every lease on the lessee's balance sheet as a right-of-use asset and a lease liability (except leases of 12 months or less and low-value assets). Tax law still splits leases into finance leases, treated as financed purchases, and operating leases, treated as true rentals. A lease is a finance lease if it transfers substantially all risks and rewards: ownership passes, a bargain purchase option, a term of about 80% or more of the asset's life, minimum payments close to fair value, or a specialised asset.

In plain words

A lease lets one party (the lessee) use an asset owned by another (the lessor) in return for rentals. For tax the key question is who owns the asset in substance. In a finance lease the lessee carries the risks and rewards of ownership, so the lessee claims capital allowances and only the interest in each payment is the lessor's income. In an operating lease the lessor keeps ownership, claims the allowances and is taxed on the whole rental.

Other lease types in the syllabus
  • Sale and leaseback: the owner sells an asset and leases it back. It releases cash; if the leaseback is a finance lease the seller keeps claiming allowances, and the instalments are deductible.
  • Leveraged lease: three parties: a lender funds most of the asset's price, alongside the lessor and lessee.
  • Sales-type lease: a manufacturer or dealer leases its own product, so it recognises a selling profit or loss when the lease starts.
  • Sub-lease: the original lessee leases the asset on to a third party, becoming a lessor for that sub-lease while staying a lessee under the head lease.
  • Leasing as a business: letting movable property for hire, with or without an option to purchase, covering rentals, operating and finance leases and hire purchase.

  • COVID-19 rent concessions (May 2020): a lessee may treat a pandemic rent concession as not being a lease modification, if the new rent is substantially the same or lower, only payments due by 30 June 2021 are reduced, and other terms do not change substantively.
  • Interest rate benchmark (IBOR) reform, phase 2: when a lease's reference rate (such as NIBOR) is replaced on an economically equivalent basis, the lessee simply updates the discount rate instead of treating it as a modification; hedge relationships are not discontinued.

  • Lease liability in a sale and leaseback (Sept 2022, effective 2024): the seller-lessee measures the lease liability from the lease payments, not the sale proceeds, so it recognises no gain on the right of use it keeps.

Chapter 13 · NTA ss.17, 19

Non-resident companies, branches and subsidiaries

A company is resident in Nigeria if it is incorporated or registered here. The old place-of-management test is gone, so dual residence now arises only when another country uses management and control; the treaty tie-breaker (place of incorporation, else mutual agreement) resolves it.

In plain words

Nigeria taxes a Nigerian company on its worldwide profits but a foreign company only on profits that come from Nigeria. The hard question is when a foreign company has enough presence here to be taxable. The main test is a permanent establishment (PE): a fixed place of business, a dependent agent, a project, or services delivered here. A Nigerian company's branch abroad and its subsidiary abroad are taxed very differently, and examiners test that difference often.

When a non-resident company is taxable in Nigeria
  1. Through a permanent establishment (fixed base)
  2. Through a dependent agent who habitually concludes contracts
  3. Supervisory activities lasting more than three months
  4. A turnkey project (taxed wholly in Nigeria, never split into "offshore" and local)
  5. Professional, consultancy, management or technical services in Nigeria
  6. Dividends, interest, rent and royalties (WHT is the final tax)
  7. A contract awarded to a Nigerian company and subcontracted to it
  8. Significant economic presence, including digital services (see next section)
  9. Transactions with connected persons not at arm's length (adjusted)
What a PE includes under the NTA
  • A place through which the business is wholly or partly carried on
  • Business run through a Nigerian company or persons it controls
  • A stock of goods from which deliveries are made
  • Projects: surveys, design, delivery, construction, installation, commissioning, decommissioning and supervision, however split among entities
  • Services rendered through employees, agents or subcontractors

Not a PE: a facility used solely for storage or display, or solely to collect information. A sales outlet is always a fixed base.

New duties: register, file returns even where WHT was deducted, keep Nigeria-specific records, and appoint a tax representative where required. A non-resident giving technical, consulting or management services to a labelled startup suffers 5% WHT as final tax.

Chapter 14 · NTA ss.17(9), 35

Taxing the digital economy

In plain words

Digital businesses can earn from Nigerian customers with no office or staff here, so the physical-presence rules miss them. Significant economic presence (SEP) closes the gap: a foreign company becomes taxable when its digital or service activity aimed at Nigeria is large or targeted enough, even without a physical presence. The topic also covers why digital trade is hard to tax and the OECD's global responses.

FA 2019 + CIT (Significant Economic Presence) Order 2020

Objective: bring foreign companies earning from Nigeria without a physical presence into the tax net; the Order defines SEP under s.13(2)(c) and (e) CITA.

Digital SEP: ₦25m or more a year from streaming or downloading content, transmitting Nigerian users' data, selling goods or services through a platform, or intermediation; or a .ng domain or Nigerian-registered website; or purposeful and sustained targeting of Nigerians (Naira pricing or billing).

Technical, professional, management, consultancy SEP: any income or payment from a Nigerian resident, or a fixed base or agent in Nigeria.

Excluded: companies covered by a multilateral digital tax agreement; payments to employees, for teaching, or by a Nigerian company's foreign fixed base.

NTA 2025

A non-resident has SEP where it transmits signals, data, images or messages to Nigeria for e-commerce, app stores, high-frequency trading, data storage, online adverts, participative platforms, online payments, user data, search engines, digital content, gaming, cloud computing or online teaching, and profit can be attributed. "Technical services" means advertising, training or supply of personnel.

Digital assets (crypto, NFTs, virtual property) are chargeable assets; gains are taxed at 30% and losses are ring-fenced. Expanded PE rules cover remote and digital activity.

Challenges (May 2025, 7 marks)
  • Cross-border nature: where value is created vs consumed
  • Valuing digital assets
  • Computing gains and deductions on them
  • Disputes over these issues
  • Tracking transactions, worsened by CBN crypto restrictions
  • Business models with no physical presence
  • Leakage of direct and indirect taxes on e-commerce
  • Outdated corporate tax rules
  • Uncertain liability of foreign companies
  • VAT missed when supply is not deemed in Nigeria
Ways of tackling them (9 marks)
  • Expand the PE definition to a digital PE
  • Dedicated digital-tax legislation rather than stretching old laws
  • NRS partnerships with payment and platform providers
  • WHT on payments to non-residents regardless of presence
  • VAT by the Nigerian recipient on cross-border services (Vodacom v FIRS)
  • Assess foreign ride-hailing apps
  • Automation of tax processes (NTP)
  • Better data handling and protection
  • Follow OECD Pillar One (taxing rights) and Pillar Two (15% global minimum)

Comparators: the EU's 2018 proposal for a 3% digital services tax on groups above €750m global and €50m EU digital revenue, and a virtual PE at €7m revenue, 100,000 users or 3,000 business contracts in a state; India's 6% equalisation levy (2016).

Pitfalls

Nov 2021: objectives of the FA 2019 and SEP Order provisions. May 2025: stating the FA 2019 provisions on SEP. Learn the ₦25m threshold, the three digital tests, and the "any income" test for technical services word for word.

Chapter 15 · CITA ss.44–46 (old); NTA ss.120–122

Treaties and double taxation relief

A double taxation agreement binds Nigeria only after signature, Federal Executive Council approval and ratification by both chambers of the National Assembly. It takes effect from 1 January of the year after it enters into force. Relief can be unilateral (domestic law), bilateral (a DTA) or multilateral (the OECD Multilateral Instrument).

In plain words

Double taxation happens when the same income is taxed twice, usually in the country where it is earned and again where the earner is resident. Double taxation relief removes or reduces the second charge. It comes from Nigerian law alone (unilateral relief) or from a double taxation agreement (DTA) between two countries, which also decides which country may tax each kind of income and caps withholding tax. In a computation Nigeria taxes the foreign income, then gives a credit for the foreign tax within a limit.

Chapter 16 · TP Regulations 2018; NTA ss.191–192, 202

Transfer pricing

In plain words

Transfer pricing is the price charged between companies in the same group for goods, services, loans or royalties. Because one owner controls both sides, prices can be set to move profit to wherever tax is lowest. The arm's length principle requires the price that unrelated parties would have agreed. The TP Regulations 2018 set out the methods, documents and penalties that enforce it.

Objectives of the Regulations
  1. Tax Nigeria's fair share of the economic activity done here, including with related persons
  2. Give the authority tools against over- and under-pricing
  3. Reduce economic double taxation
  4. A level field between MNEs and independent firms
  5. Certainty for taxpayers
Scope: controlled transactions
  • Sale and purchase of goods and services
  • Sale, purchase or lease of tangible assets
  • Transfer, licence or use of intangibles
  • Provision of services
  • Lending and borrowing
  • Manufacturing arrangements
  • Anything else affecting profit or loss

Commenced 12 March 2018 (2012 Regulations from 2 August 2012). The Study Text cites documentation for connected transactions of ₦300m or more a year.

Methods

Traditional transaction: comparable uncontrolled price; resale price (resale price less an arm's-length gross margin); cost plus.

Transactional profit: transactional net margin (net margin on costs, sales or assets vs comparables); profit split (combined profit shared as independents would).

Comparability factors: characteristics of the goods or services; functional analysis (functions, assets, risks); contract terms; economic circumstances; business strategies.

Connected persons (s.202 NTA)

Relatives and spouses; trustee and settlor; partners; a person and a company he or relatives control; companies under common control; one company participating in another's management, control or capital; persons acting on another's directions or with practical control. Not connected merely as employee or client. New special cases: digital platforms coordinating pricing, an SEP and its foreign principal, IP licensor and licensee, JV partners contributing 25% or more.

s.191: transactions between connected persons not at arm's length are artificial and may be disregarded. s.192: related-party arrangements must be at arm's length and reported.

Documents (pitfall May 2023: the disclosure form)

Declaration form (by 30 June, for the previous year): reporting entity; immediate parent; directors; major shareholders and related parties; ownership structure; subsidiaries and connected persons; external auditors; tax consultant; company secretary; declarant; declaration.

Disclosure form (by 30 September): reporting entity; income from controlled transactions; costs of controlled transactions; summary of transactions with connected persons; TP method and documentation; basic financials of the entity and group; declarant; declaration.

Also: TP policy (group or local, forward-looking) and a TP compliance report. The burden of proving arm's length is on the taxpayer.

Chapters 17, 24

BEPS, CbCR, thin capitalisation, treaty shopping, tax havens

Base erosion shrinks the taxable profit reported in a country; profit shifting moves profit to a low-tax country. MNE groups dominate because they have the network of entities, the capital to build structures and the profits to pay for advice.

In plain words

Base erosion and profit shifting (BEPS) describes how multinational groups cut their tax by moving profit from high-tax countries to low-tax ones, using gaps and mismatches between countries' rules. The OECD and G20 answered in 2015 with 15 actions to close those gaps. Thin capitalisation, treaty shopping and tax havens are three of the main tools; country-by-country reporting is one of the main remedies.

Techniques, not examples (Nov 2021)
  1. Trademark and technology licensing: IP parked in a low-tax entity charging royalties to the group
  2. Thin capitalisation: subsidiaries funded with debt, so interest strips profit
  3. Hybrid mismatch arrangements: instruments or entities treated differently by two countries, giving double non-taxation or double deduction
  4. Assets in entities without substance in preferential regimes

Contributing factors: the digital economy and weak international cooperation.

OECD's 15 actions
  1. Digital economy: identify how digital business defeats existing rules and develop options covering direct and indirect tax (now Pillars One and Two).
  2. Hybrid mismatches: neutralise instruments and entities treated differently by two countries, which give double non-taxation, double deductions or long deferral.
  3. CFC rules: strengthen controlled foreign company rules so profits parked in low-tax subsidiaries are taxed in the parent's country.
  4. Interest deductions: limit excessive interest on related- and third-party debt (the source of Nigeria's 30% of EBITDA cap).
  5. Harmful tax practices: require substantial activity for preferential regimes and exchange rulings spontaneously.
  6. Treaty abuse: model treaty provisions and domestic rules to deny treaty benefits in inappropriate cases such as treaty shopping.
  7. Artificial avoidance of PE status: widen the PE definition to catch commissionaire arrangements and split activities.
  8. TP – intangibles: align returns from intangibles with where value is created.
  9. TP – risks and capital: stop profit following risks or capital allocated by contract alone.
  10. TP – other high-risk transactions: recharacterise transactions independents would not undertake; guidance on profit splits and management fees.
  11. Measuring BEPS: collect and analyse data on its scale and on the effect of the actions.
  12. Mandatory disclosure: require taxpayers and advisers to disclose aggressive tax planning arrangements.
  13. TP documentation and CbCR: master file, local file and country-by-country report to give tax authorities a full picture.
  14. Dispute resolution: make mutual agreement procedures faster and more effective.
  15. Multilateral instrument: one convention that amends many bilateral treaties at once to apply the treaty-related actions.
Country-by-country reporting
  • Ultimate parent resident in Nigeria with consolidated group revenue of ₦160bn or more files a CbC report within 12 months of year end.
  • Late filing: ₦10m, then ₦1m a month.
  • Every resident constituent entity notifies the NRS whether it is the UPE or surrogate, or names the reporting entity, by the last day of the reporting year.
  • OECD Action 13 guidance governs gaps.
Thin capitalisation

Deliberately high gearing so interest deductions shift profit. Harms creditors (more risk) and tax authorities (lost base). Nigeria's rule since FA 2019 and kept by the NTA: interest to foreign connected lenders capped at 30% of EBITDA, excess carried 5 years; banks and insurers excluded (pitfall May 2023).

Treaty shopping

A resident of a third country routes income through a treaty country to claim benefits not meant for it (May 2024). Countries curb it with beneficial-ownership tests, limitation-on-benefits clauses, the principal purpose test (MLI), substance requirements and domestic anti-avoidance rules. The NTA grants treaty benefits only to a resident beneficial owner.

Tax havens (pitfall Nov 2020: OECD factors)
  • No or only nominal tax on the relevant income
  • Laws or practices that block effective exchange of information
  • Lack of transparency in legislative, legal or administrative provisions
  • No requirement for substantial local activity

Types: primary, semi and conduit havens. Uses: personal residency, corporate residency, asset holding through trusts and companies.

Measures against havens (May 2025)
  • CFC rules taxing undistributed haven profits
  • Transfer pricing and thin capitalisation rules
  • Exit charges on emigrating individuals, trusts and companies
  • Automatic exchange of information (CRS, FATCA)
  • Black and grey lists; defensive WHT
  • Substance and beneficial-ownership requirements

Regional blocs and international tax policy

In plain words

Regional integration is when neighbouring countries agree to trade and cooperate more closely through shared rules and institutions. It comes in stages, from a free trade area up to a full economic union, and each stage changes tariffs and tax policy. ECOWAS and the African Continental Free Trade Area (AfCFTA) are the blocs that matter for Nigeria. International tax policy asks the wider question of how countries share taxing rights and cooperate.

Pitfall: Nov 2020 Q3

Candidates could not separate a common market from an economic union. The common market frees labour and capital; the economic union adds harmonised economic policy on top. Also distinguish regional integration (states sharing institutions and rules, which may be political or environmental) from a trade bloc (a free-trade zone formed by tariff and trade agreements).

ECOWAS & the common external tariff

Treaty signed in Lagos on 25 May 1975. Aims: suppress customs duties, create a CET, harmonise economic and financial policies, form a monetary zone. CET adopted 25 October 2013 with five bands (0% essential social goods, 5% primary necessities and raw materials, 10% intermediate goods, 20% final consumer goods, 35% specific goods for economic development) on the HS 2012 nomenclature.

Trade defence measures (pitfall May 2024): safeguard measures; anti-dumping measures; anti-subsidy and countervailing measures; supplementary protection measures.

AfCFTA

55 countries. Objectives: a single continental market, more intra-African trade, competitiveness, integration, sustainable development. Runs through protocols (goods, services, dispute settlement), instruments (rules of origin, tariff concessions, a non-tariff-barrier reporting tool) and institutions (Assembly, Council of Ministers, Committee of Senior Trade Officials, technical committees).

Challenges: lost tariff revenue, adjustment costs, competition for small firms, implementation, non-tariff barriers, infrastructure, institutional capacity. Against ECOWAS in trade in goods (pitfall May 2021): AfCFTA is a continental free trade area liberalising tariffs under rules of origin; ECOWAS is a customs union with a CET and its own trade liberalisation scheme.

Benefits / costs of blocs

Benefits: trade creation, jobs, easier consensus, certainty for investors. Costs: trade diversion to less efficient members, employment shifts, loss of sovereignty.

Organisations shaping tax policy (pitfall May 2026, Nov 2024)
  • OECD: Model Convention, BEPS, Global Forum, TP guidelines
  • UN: Model Convention for developing countries; UN tax convention work
  • IMF: policy advice and technical assistance
  • World Bank: Global Tax Program, financing reforms
  • ATAF: African tax administrations' cooperation
  • WTO/GATT: trade rules, non-discrimination, customs valuation
  • EU, AU, ECOWAS, WCO
Information exchange & neutrality

Exchange on request, automatic (CRS; Nigeria signed the MCAA in August 2017) and spontaneous. Nigeria's framework sits under the Data Protection Act (adequacy, consent, AG whitelist). Neutrality (Nov 2023): a neutral tax does not change behaviour. Applications: broaden the base and lower rates; prefer credits to deductions when encouraging behaviour; tax undesired activities deliberately.

Jurisdiction rests on residence (worldwide income) or source (income earned within borders). Its limits are geographical, subject-matter and personal. International tax policy aims at fairness, efficiency, neutrality, competitiveness and revenue.

Other blocs the syllabus compares
  • European Union: 27 states with common economic, social and security policies. Goals: peace and citizens' well-being; sustainable growth with price stability and full employment; fighting social exclusion; scientific progress; economic, social and territorial cohesion.
  • NAFTA (US, Canada, Mexico, 1994): a free trade area aiming at freer trade and wider choice, lower prices and better quality, stronger health and safety standards, economic stability, and a market driven by supply and demand rather than barriers.
  • Why AfCFTA differs: it is continental and still building its rules of origin and tariff schedules, while the EU is an economic union and NAFTA a mature free trade area.

International initiatives, SDGs and ESG

In plain words

This part tests awareness of recent developments rather than computation. It covers international cooperation on enforcement (joint audits, information exchange, capacity building), how technology is changing tax work, and how tax supports the UN Sustainable Development Goals and responsible ESG conduct in the extractive industries. Answers are lists of explained points, so clear headings and one point per mark matter most.

Tax Inspectors Without Borders

An OECD and UNDP initiative that builds audit capacity in developing countries. Serving or recently retired audit experts work alongside local officials on live audits, including TP and sector cases. The host country leads and sets the scope; experts never replace local staff.

Cooperative compliance (pitfall Nov 2025)

A relationship between the revenue authority and, usually, a large taxpayer based on trust, transparency and mutual understanding. The taxpayer runs a sound tax control framework and discloses risks early; the authority gives timely, certain responses. Result: fewer disputes, lower costs, earlier certainty.

Simultaneous tax examination

Two or more countries examine the same taxpayer at the same time, each independently, and exchange information. It encourages compliance, exposes evasion and fosters cooperation, but needs careful coordination, protects confidentiality and copes with complex laws. Joint audits go a step further.

Also in this chapter
  • Common Reporting Standard (CRS): banks collect data on accounts held by foreign tax residents and send it to their tax authority, which passes it to the residents' home countries every year. Nigeria signed the Multilateral Competent Authority Agreement in August 2017, so it now receives data on Nigerians' accounts in more than 95 jurisdictions, including Bermuda, the Cayman Islands, Luxembourg and Mauritius, and Nigerian banks report foreigners' accounts in return.
  • FATCA (US, 2010, part of the HIRE Act): US citizens must report foreign accounts and assets; foreign financial institutions must report US account holders to the IRS (directly or under an intergovernmental agreement) or face exclusion and penalties. It shows how one country's rules reach banks worldwide.
  • Mutual agreement procedure (MAP): under a treaty, a Nigerian taxpayer taxed contrary to the treaty asks the competent authority (the Minister of Finance or delegate) to negotiate a solution with the other country.
  • Disruptive technologies: digital platforms and app stores make transactions hard to track; AI and data analytics help authorities spot risk but need investment and skills; blockchain and crypto are hard to trace and value; cloud computing blurs where income is earned. Benefits: efficiency and better compliance. Risks: data security. The future is "TaxTech" built through collaboration between authorities and the private sector.
Technology in tax practice (May 2022, May 2023)
  • Cloud tax management platforms and e-filing (the NRS e-filing system)
  • AI and robotic process automation for data collation and analysis
  • Data analytics and visualisation for risk review
  • E-invoicing and fiscalisation links

Impact: speed, accuracy, real-time compliance, new advisory lines. Challenges: cost, cyber risk, skills gaps, unreliable power and internet.

Taxation and the SDGs (May 2026)

Roles: raise revenue for development spending; redistribute income through progressive taxes; change behaviour (tax pollution, reward clean activity); promote growth through targeted incentives; fund public goods such as health, education and infrastructure.

Strategies: green taxes (carbon), incentives for sustainable investment, progressive taxation.

Challenges and mitigation in Nigeria: limited tax capacity → invest in administration and technology; evasion and avoidance → transparency and information exchange; digital economy → SEP and digital rules; the informal sector → simplified regimes and registration drives; weak revenue mobilisation → broaden the base, fewer exemptions. UNDP's Tax for SDGs initiative supplies an assessment tool and runs TIWB with the OECD.

ESG in the extractive industries

Penalties for non-compliance: fines (the Climate Change Act 2021 targets net zero by 2050–2070, adds a carbon tax and requires a sustainability officer); litigation and regulatory audits; loss of licences; reputational damage; supply chain and operational disruption. Disputes can go to ADR.

EITI Standard: disclosure of revenue collection and allocation, contracts and licences, beneficial ownership, production, social spending, state-owned enterprises, commodity trading, open data. The 2023 Standard adds energy transition, corruption risk, gender equity and revenue collection.

Sustainable practices: benefits are competitive advantage, motivated staff, profitability, reputation, resource efficiency; challenges are inconsistent regulation, low awareness and cost.

The 17 Sustainable Development Goals (UN, 2015)

No poverty · Zero hunger · Good health and well-being · Quality education · Gender equality · Clean water and sanitation · Affordable and clean energy · Decent work and economic growth · Industry, innovation and infrastructure · Reduced inequalities · Sustainable cities and communities · Responsible consumption and production · Climate action · Life below water · Life on land · Peace, justice and strong institutions · Partnerships for the goals.

Principles: intergenerational equity and environmental stewardship. Achieved through renewable energy, sustainable agriculture and responsible consumption. A fourth taxation strategy is tax transparency, so taxes are seen to fund development.

UNDP Tax for SDGs (2021): helps countries use tax to mobilise domestic resources; offers an SDG taxation framework and assessment tool and an annual Tax for SDGs Dialogue; runs TIWB with the OECD (over $2bn extra revenue raised since 2015); covered 25 countries in 2022. Expected results: more domestic revenue, stronger administration against evasion and illicit flows, tax policy aligned with the SDGs, digitalised tax services and gender-equal outcomes.

Why ESG matters in the extractive industries

Need: risk management, long-term sustainability, stakeholder expectations, regulation (NESREA, NUPRC, NEITI) and brand value.

Environmental compliance: environmental impact assessments, waste management, oil-spill response plans, biodiversity protection. Social: community engagement, livelihood restoration with fair compensation, social investment, cultural heritage protection. Governance: transparency, anti-corruption policies, board oversight.

Benefits of sustainable practice: reputation, cost savings, innovation, risk management, access to capital, talent, avoiding "greenwashing". Challenges: high initial cost, complex supply chains, varied regulation, aligning stakeholders, measuring impact, short- vs long-term pressure. In plain words

A merger joins two or more companies into one; an acquisition is one company buying control of another. Under the normal rules such deals would trigger cessation of one business, commencement of another, gains on the assets transferred and VAT. The law relieves genuine reorganisations, especially between related companies, so tax does not block a commercial combination, but it attaches conditions and needs the tax authority's clearance.

Concessions: no commencement or cessation rules; assets deemed transferred at residue the day after transfer; no VAT and no gain on transfers between related parties.

  • No merger, takeover, transfer or restructuring may go ahead without the authority's consent or direction.
  • Clearance of all taxes due, including on gains, must be obtained first.
  • The authority may give directions, including security for tax.

Basis periods: a merger is neither commencement nor cessation, so preceding year basis continues; a brand-new surviving company starts its first period from commencement. Upstream petroleum: the acquirer's period starts on the transfer date.

Concepts and types
  • Merger: the assets, liabilities and businesses of two or more companies are vested in one company, which may or may not be one of them.
  • Acquisition: one company buys all or most of another's equity; above 50%, the target becomes its subsidiary.
  • Horizontal merger: companies in the same line of business (Chartered Bank and IBTC became Stanbic IBTC).
  • Vertical merger: a supplier and its customer in the same industry (a soft-drink maker and a bottle maker).
Regulators and filings
  • FCCPC: the main M&A regulator under the Federal Competition and Consumer Protection Act; reviews competition effects.
  • SEC: deals involving public companies: fairness, transparency, investor protection; annual, quarterly and material-event filings.
  • CAC: annual returns and notices of changes in directors and shareholding.
  • CBN and NAICOM: approve deals in banking and insurance.
  • Tax authorities (NRS, State IRS, FCT-IRS): notify any change in particulars within 30 days (s.9 NTAA) and obtain clearance.

On a takeover by a new company, also mention cessation of the old trade, treatment of allowances and losses, stamp duties on transfers, and VAT and WHT on advisers' fees.

Chapter 24 · NTA s.191; NTAA s.46

Planning, avoidance and evasion

In plain words

Tax planning, avoidance and evasion sit on a scale. Planning is arranging your affairs lawfully to pay less tax, in the way the law intends. Avoidance is legal on its face but uses gaps against the law's purpose, and the tax authority may set it aside. Evasion is illegal: hiding income, inflating costs or failing to file. Questions ask you to define each, give examples and state the consequences.

Tax planning

Lawfully arranging affairs to reduce tax within the letter and spirit of the law: timing asset purchases and disposals, using incentives (EDI, free zones), roll-over or reinvestment exemptions, meeting deadlines to avoid penalties, choosing structure and location.

Tax avoidance

Legal but against the intent of the law, exploiting gaps (artificial transactions, thin capitalisation, treaty shopping). Countered by specific anti-avoidance rules (certain, but narrow) and a general rule (fewer laws, wider reach). Mandatory disclosure of arrangements that give a relief, a repayment or avoid a charge.

Tax evasion

Illegal: understating income, overstating expenses, fictitious transactions, failure to file, diverting WHT or VAT collected. Leads to back duty assessments with no time limit, penalties, interest and prosecution.

Pitfall: Nov 2021 Q3

Candidates defined the concepts but not their tax implications. Planning: lower legitimate tax, no penalty. Avoidance: transactions disregarded under s.191, TP adjustments, interest disallowed, additional assessments. Evasion: penalties, interest, prosecution, unlimited look-back.

Tax planning checklist (Study Text 24.2)
  • Timing of asset purchases, disposals and capital allowance claims.
  • Hiring instead of buying assets: the full hire charge is deductible.
  • Specific rather than general provisions, so they are deductible.
  • PAYE and WHT deducted correctly, to avoid penalties.
  • Critical dates: returns, objections, PAYE, WHT, VAT, NSITF.
  • Gains: use reinvestment reliefs and exemption thresholds.
  • Incentives and exemptions: economic development credit, free zones, agricultural exemption, NTA Chapter 8 exemptions, government bonds.
  • Remuneration structure and benefits in kind.
Anti-avoidance in the NTAA 2025
  • Mandatory disclosure (s.30): anyone entering a transaction whose main purpose is a tax advantage must disclose it. A tax advantage includes a relief, a repayment, avoiding or reducing a charge or assessment, deferring payment, or avoiding a duty to deduct tax.
  • Artificial transactions (s.46): the authority may disregard artificial or fictitious transactions; deals between connected persons not on arm's-length terms are deemed artificial.
  • Prohibited avoidance arrangements (s.47): a general rule. Where the main purpose is a tax benefit contrary to the law's object, the authority may adjust, disregard or recharacterise the arrangement.
  • Specific vs general rules: specific rules are certain and easy to amend but make the law complex and invite new loopholes; general rules are fewer and catch future schemes but are vague and depend on officials' discretion.
  • Case law: Lord Clyde in Ayrshire Pullman v CIR (no one must arrange affairs to give the Revenue "the largest possible shovel"); Belgore CJ in FBIR v American International Insurance Co ("Tax is an obligation, not a duty").

Non-tax factors in choosing a location (May 2023, six marks): political and economic stability; physical, legal and financial infrastructure; limited exchange controls; access to markets and raw materials; skilled labour; banking and communications; language and legal system; investment protection. Direct taxes (income tax, CIT, HT) are borne by the person who pays them; indirect taxes (VAT, customs and excise, stamp duties) are passed on in the price.

Chapters 23, 25, 26, 27

Practice management, audit and investigation, ethics, tax accounting

In plain words

This section covers the professional side of tax work: the legal and ethical duties of an accountant in tax practice, how to take on and manage clients, and how tax authorities audit and investigate taxpayers. It also covers how tax appears in financial statements, including deferred tax. These are discussion questions, marked one point per mark.

Running a tax practice (pitfall May 2026: legal requirements)

Legal requirement: under ICAN's code, a chartered accountant in public practice must not engage in any business or activity that impairs, or might impair, integrity, objectivity or the profession's reputation. A practitioner acting for reward must be accredited as a tax practitioner (NTP; JRB guidance on accreditation).

Engagement: client's appointment letter → engagement letter (scope, deliverables, fees) → introduction letter to the tax office.

Risks: lack of expertise, lack of attention to detail, poor documentation, unpredictable income, technology and cyber risk, liability for wrong advice.

Benefits: own schedule, financial upside, choice of clients, your own vision.

Mitigation: work only within competence; quality control and review; documented filing positions; engagement letters; professional indemnity insurance; continuing education; regular updates to strategy as laws change.

New client files (Nov 2024)

From the client: certificate of incorporation, CAC forms (status report, allotment, registered office), memorandum and articles, TIN and registration evidence, audited accounts, prior tax computations, assessments, receipts, WHT credit notes and tax clearance certificates.

Kept by the consultant: engagement letter, permanent file of client details, correspondence with the tax office, computations and working papers, objections and appeals history, payment and clearance records.

Disclosure to third parties only with client consent, when required by law (court order, tax authority notice), in a professional duty (disciplinary or quality review, defending yourself), or in the public interest (fraud, money laundering).

Tax audit

Stages: selection → preliminary review of the file (ratios: liquidity, efficiency, gearing, profitability) → notification → pre-audit meeting → field audit → interim reports → post-audit review → reconciliation meetings → final report and additional assessments → objections.

Objectives: confirm returns are correct, enforce compliance, detect under-declaration, educate taxpayers, refer frauds for investigation, deter non-filing.

NRS document schedule (pitfall Nov 2023): audited accounts, management accounts, chart of accounts, trial balances, general ledger, fixed asset register, staff and payroll lists, WHT and VAT schedules with receipts, contracts and agreements, bank statements, prior computations and assessments, TP documentation.

Investigation and back duty

Pre-investigation meeting (Nov 2024): explain the purpose, confirm background facts, agree the scope and timetable, request documents, hear the taxpayer's side, seek cooperation.

Stages: surveillance → notification → evidential audit → case preparation → arraignment; terminated if evidence is thin or no crime is shown.

Back duty (Nov 2021, May 2025): an audit of past returns where errors, omissions or deliberate understatement are suspected; covers up to 6 years, unlimited for fraud; records re-examined and tax recomputed; shortfall assessed with penalty and interest; objection and appeal rights apply.

Powers: notices to file, attend, produce books and give access; distraint (goods held 14 days before sale; land only with a High Court order); search and seizure on reasonable suspicion.

Ethics (pitfall Nov 2023, May 2024)

IESBA fundamental principles: integrity, objectivity, professional competence and due care, confidentiality, professional behaviour.

Threats: self-interest, self-review, advocacy, familiarity, intimidation. Safeguards: from the profession or legislation (education, CPD, disciplinary regimes, reviews), from the firm (quality control, rotation, independent review, fee policies) and in the engagement (second partner review, declining work, documenting judgements).

Punitive measures (May 2022): ICAN disciplinary tribunal (reprimand, suspension, expulsion), penalties under the tax laws for aiding offences, criminal prosecution.

Tax accounting & deferred tax (pitfall Nov 2022)

Deferred tax arises from temporary differences, mostly carrying amount vs tax written-down value.

  • Deferred tax liability = (carrying amount − TWDV) × 30%
  • Deferred tax asset = (TWDV − carrying amount) × 30%
  • Charge in profit or loss = current CIT + development levy ± prior-year adjustments + movement in deferred tax

Compute depreciation correctly before anything else: the 2022 report says that is exactly where candidates failed. Disclose current and deferred tax separately; misleading disclosure risks penalties, qualified opinions and reputational damage.

The five threats, defined
  • Self-interest: a financial or other interest improperly influences judgement, such as undue dependence on one client's fees.
  • Self-review: the accountant will not properly evaluate their own, or their firm's, earlier work.
  • Advocacy: promoting a client's position so far that objectivity is lost.
  • Familiarity: a long or close relationship makes the accountant too sympathetic or too accepting.
  • Intimidation: actual or perceived pressure deters objective action.

Conflicts of interest arise when two clients' interests conflict on a matter, or the practitioner's own interest conflicts with a client's. Identify, disclose, get consent or decline, and document.

NOCLAR: suspected breach of tax law
  1. Become aware (see it, but do not go looking for it).
  2. Understand the matter and the law involved.
  3. Raise it with management or those charged with governance.
  4. Assess their response.
  5. Decide further action: report to an appropriate authority or withdraw from the engagement.
  6. Document the matter, discussions and decisions.

Employed accountants first follow internal protocols, escalating above a superior who is involved; senior accountants carry wider duties. Sanctions for breaches include fines, reprimand, suspension, expulsion and withdrawal of the practising licence.

Data privacy (Nigeria Data Protection Act 2023)
  • Lawful, fair and transparent processing with informed consent.
  • Purpose limitation and data minimisation.
  • Accuracy and storage limitation.
  • Confidentiality and integrity: protection by design, impact assessments for high-risk processing, breach notification.
  • Data subjects' rights to information, access and rectification.
  • Oversight: the Nigeria Data Protection Commission registers major controllers and imposes penalties.
Technology in practice and its limits
  • Cloud tax platforms: no servers to buy or maintain; access anywhere.
  • AI and robotic process automation: collate and analyse data faster with fewer errors.
  • Blockchain: tamper-proof transaction records.
  • Benefits to taxpayers: easier compliance, lower cost, efficiency, compliance without physical contact.
  • Challenges: poor connectivity and power, cybersecurity fears, digital-skills gaps, cost, systems not flexible enough for changing laws.
  • Objections may now be served by courier or electronically (FA 2019 and 2020; NTAA 2025).
The tax account in the books (Study Text 27.2–27.3)

The tax account opens with unpaid tax brought forward and is credited with this year's provisions for income tax (Dr profit or loss) and development levy, plus any prior-year under-provision; it is debited with over-provisions, cash paid and WHT credit notes set off (Dr tax account, Cr WHT receivable). The closing balance is the tax payable in the statement of financial position. Deferred tax is posted to its own account. Wrong or misleading tax disclosure risks penalties, qualified audit opinions and loss of credibility.

Chapter 28 · PIA 2021; NTA ss.68–122

Oil and gas: hydrocarbon tax and upstream CIT

Converted leases pay hydrocarbon tax and CIT; unconverted OPLs and OMLs stay under petroleum profits tax until they expire or convert. The NTA now houses all three charges; the PIA keeps licensing, royalty, host communities and conversion.

In plain words

Oil and gas profits are taxed under special rules because they are large and come from a national resource. Under the Petroleum Industry Act 2021, an upstream company with a converted lease pays two taxes: hydrocarbon tax on profit from crude oil and associated liquids, and ordinary company income tax. Hydrocarbon tax has its own allowable-cost list, a cap on total costs (the 65% cost-price ratio) and a per-barrel production allowance. Leases not yet converted stay under the older petroleum profits tax.

Who pays what
  • HT rate (s.72): 30% for petroleum mining leases (PML), onshore and shallow water; 15% for petroleum prospecting licences (PPL) and marginal fields.
  • CIT: 30% on all upstream operations, plus development levy 4% of assessable profit.
  • PPT (unconverted): 85% of chargeable profit; 65.75% for up to 5 years before pre-production costs are amortised.
  • Deep offshore (water deeper than 200m): no HT, CIT only. Frontier acreage: no HT until reclassified.
  • HT is charged only on crude oil, field condensates and NGLs from associated gas upstream of the measurement point, plus balancing charges. Not on associated gas itself or non-associated gas and its liquids.
Conversion and transition
  • 18-month window from 16 August 2021 for voluntary conversion; conversion is automatic on expiry (Opobo: OML expired 31 Dec 2022, PML from 1 Jan 2023).
  • Year of conversion: PPT from 1 January to the conversion date, PIA after it.
  • Mixed licences: one return with three computations (PPT, HT, CIT).
  • PPT losses and unrecouped allowances carry into HT and CIT; investment tax credit does not.
  • Accounting period is the calendar year; the first starts on the first sale or bulk disposal. HT is on actual year basis, paid in 12 monthly estimates from March with a 13th balancing payment within 21 days of assessment.
Allowed for HT (s.68 NTA / s.263 PIA)
  • Royalties incurred and paid; production-sharing payments to the Federation Account
  • Concession rentals under the lease or licence
  • Production costs; terminalling
  • Repairs of plant, machinery and fixtures; renewal, repair or alteration of production implements and utensils
  • Drilling costs, tangible and intangible, of the first exploration well and first two appraisal wells in a field (later wells are qualifying drilling expenditure for allowances)
  • Gas reinjection wells (ratified by NUPRC)
  • Levies, stamp duties and fees to any government (State ground rent)
  • Acquisition of geophysical, geological and geochemical data
  • Decommissioning and abandonment fund contributions (deductible only with at least 15% in a Nigerian-bank escrow)
  • Host Community Development Trust (3% of prior-year opex), Environmental Remediation Fund, NDDC
  • Excess cost brought forward under the CPR
Disallowed for HT (s.69 NTA)
  • Purchase of information on deposits (other than geological and geophysical data)
  • Penalties and gas flaring fees
  • Finance and bank charges, interest, arbitration and litigation costs, bad debts
  • Head office, affiliate and shared costs; R&D; shared indirect costs
  • Production bonuses, signature bonuses, lease or licence fees, assignment fees
  • Tax grossed up or borne for contractors
  • Capital, improvements, depreciation
  • Insurance recoveries; non-operational premises
  • Income taxes and development levy
  • Unapproved pension contributions; donations

For upstream CIT the list is wider: indirect and above-field costs, and interest, are allowed under ordinary rules; the CPR does not apply.

Chapter 29 · NMMA 2007; NTA s.64, Eighth Schedule; NEITI Act 2007

Mining

Mining companies pay CIT at 30% on preceding year basis and the development levy. Royalty is now imposed under the NTA at Eighth Schedule ad valorem rates (mostly 7.5%–15%) on the official or international price, is deductible, and is administered by the NRS. Contributions to environmental, rehabilitation, reclamation and closure funds are deductible if cash-backed in a dedicated account or trust managed by independent trustees.

In plain words

Solid minerals belong to the Federal Government, and companies mine them under titles issued by the Mining Cadastre Office under the Minerals and Mining Act 2007. Mining companies pay ordinary company income tax and the development levy, plus a deductible royalty on the value of minerals produced. Questions mix a computation with law points on titles, land, possession of minerals and incentives.

Incentives under the Minerals and Mining Act
  1. Tax holiday of 3 years, renewable for 2; dividends in the holiday free of WHT
  2. Retention of part of export earnings in a domiciliary account
  3. Customs and import duty exemption on mining plant (duty due if sold locally)
  4. Free transfer of foreign currency for certified loans and capital repatriation
  5. Expatriate remittance quota free of tax
  6. Accelerated allowance on mining expenditure: 95%, 5% retained (old CITA)
  7. Investment allowance 10% on plant (ended 31 Aug 2023)
  8. Approved infrastructure capitalised at 95% in year one, plus possible rural investment allowance
  9. 5% annual indexation of unused allowances (for early mines; later case by case)
  10. Royalty deferral with FEC approval
  11. Environmental and closure reserves deductible when spent and certified
Rules candidates misstated

Possession and purchase (May 2025). s.93: no one may possess a mineral unless it was won from their own title area, they hold a permit to possess or purchase, or they are an authorised agent or employee of such a person. s.94: no purchase without a licence to purchase minerals. s.95: small-scale lease output must be sold to a licensed mineral buying centre with receipts. s.96: buying centres keep records of purchases, sales and the source mine. s.92 exempts genuine specimens.

Fees (Nov 2025). Application fees and an annual service fee per cadastral unit. If unpaid, the Mining Cadastre Office gives 30 days' written notice, then records the default and revokes the title.

Priority of land (Nov 2025). Mining has priority over other land uses and is an overriding public interest under the Land Use Act. Where a lease is granted over land with an existing right of occupancy, the State Governor revokes that right within 60 days.

Administration

Mining Cadastre Office (sole licensing agency; registers for reconnaissance permits, exploration licences, mining leases, small-scale leases, quarry leases and water use permits; first come, first served by the priority register). Mines Inspectorate and Mines Environmental Compliance departments; State Mineral Resources and Environmental Management Committees. Titles: reconnaissance permit, exploration licence, small-scale mining lease, mining lease, quarry lease, water use permit. Environmental programmes are deemed approved after 60 days' silence; a second rejection can go to arbitration within 30 days.

NEITI (pitfall May 2024)

Vision: an accountable, effective, well-resourced and result-oriented NEITI. Mission: a culture of transparency, accountability, due process and zero tolerance for corruption in the extractive industries, for the benefit of citizens. Objectives: due process and transparency in payments to government; accountability in revenue receipts; eliminate corruption in determining, paying, receiving and posting revenue; transparency in how government applies the revenue; conformity with global EITI principles. Governed by a National Stakeholders Working Group (chair plus up to 14 members, quorum 8, meets quarterly).

Mining pitfalls
  • May 2022: certified exploration and processing expenditure in adjusted profit; mining incentives.
  • Nov 2023, Nov 2024: allowable and disallowable expenses for a mining company.
  • Penalties under the Act: illegal mining or false statements → licence revoked, fine of at least ₦20m and at least five years' imprisonment; ₦20,000 a day if continuing.
